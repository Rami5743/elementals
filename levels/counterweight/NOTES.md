# Counterweight (`counterweight`) — Water + Earth

**Introduces:** a gate held open by the weight of the one thing the rest of the level needs. Taking
the stone off the plate is both the only way forward and the thing that cuts the hall in two with
your partner on the far side.

## The intended solution

1. **Earth** shoves the stone left onto the heavy plate at the end of the hall. The far gate opens
   while the stone is on it — and only while.
2. **Water** walks through the gate, puts out the flames behind it, takes its gem and goes on to the
   lever. It is now on the far side of a gate that is about to shut.
3. **Earth** takes its gem, goes round to the other side of the stone and shoves it back the other
   way, out onto the grating in the middle of the hall. The gate shuts behind Water as soon as the
   plate is clear — that is the price, and it is unavoidable.
4. The stone will not fall through the grating on its own: nothing drives a stone through a one-way
   floor except a door closing on top of it. The lid above the grating is open while the lever is
   up, so **Earth** steps clear of its two tiles.
5. **Water**, stranded on the far side, throws the lever. The lid comes down, drives the stone
   through the grating into the crawl below, and the stone settles on the heavy plate down there.
   That opens the vault at the far end and the door in the crawl.
6. **Earth** drops through the grating, crawls the length of the hall underneath it, climbs out at
   the far end through the ledge, and walks through the vault to its door. Water is already at
   its own.

## Why each player is needed

- **Earth** is the only one who can move the stone, and the stone has to be in two different places
  at two different times. The plates are both heavy, so no player can stand in for it — and a player
  standing on one is a player not standing in a door.
- **Water** is the only one who can put out the flames, and the lever behind them is the only thing
  that closes the lid. Once the stone moves, that lever can only be reached from the far side, which
  is why somebody has to be stranded there on purpose.

## The insight

The gate is open because the stone is where it is. Everything else in the level wants the stone
somewhere else. So the question is not how to open the gate — it is already open — but who should
be on which side of it when it shuts, and that has to be decided several moves before it happens.

## Likely failures and how players recover

- **Moving the stone before Water has crossed.** The gate shuts with both players on the near side
  and the lever out of reach. Push the stone back onto the plate, let Water through, and start
  again: it costs the walk, nothing else.
- **Both crossing before the stone is moved.** Nobody is left who can move it. Earth can come back
  through the gate while it is still open.
- **Standing under the lid when the lever is thrown.** The lid rests on whoever is under it and the
  stone stays where it is. Step aside and throw it again.
- **Pushing the stone past the lid's two tiles.** Earth can push it back; the hall is flat.

## Shortcuts that were closed

- Both plates are heavy, so neither player can hold either of them, alone or standing on the other:
  a plate reads the largest single mass overlapping it.
- The gate, the flames and the vault are each four tiles tall under rock.
- The crawl under the hall is shut by a door until the stone lands on the plate down there, so it
  cannot be used to get past the gate early.
- `check` warns that the crawl's door can be "jumped over" from the left. It cannot: the warning
  comes from the heuristic noticing that the hall above runs past the same column while the gate up
  there is open. The two corridors never connect except through the grating.

## Kinship with the campaign

Heavy Hearted and Counterpoise are both about weight on plates, and Jack is about propping something
so it cannot close. None of them makes the weight that is holding your road open the same weight you
have to carry away, so that going on means cutting the party in half.
